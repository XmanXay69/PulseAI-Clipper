## Rules stated
- Almost all editing comes down to three choices: what to show, when to cut, what to cut to [said 00:44].
- Murch's "rule of six" is named as a ranked list of what each cut should serve. The video gives no percentages and points to a blog link for the list [said 01:24, 02:34].
- Pace follows mood: a mournful or contemplative scene holds shots longer; a frantic action scene cuts rapidly [said 01:45]. No durations given.
- Every edit should be thoughtful and purposeful, with a reason for each cut [said 02:34].
- Eyeline match: when a character notices something, cut to what they see; the character's eyes motivate the cut. Reveal can be delayed or withheld to build intrigue [said 02:55-03:39].
- Shot / reverse shot keeps scene geography clear in conversation, usually from a similar angle and composition [said 04:22].
- Insert = shot of a detail or prop, often prompted by an eyeline match, to emphasise it [said 04:42].
- Cross cutting joins two or more scenes in different places, usually simultaneous; it creates ironic contrast or keeps multi-front action moving, and can make parallels between scenes that would not exist otherwise [said 05:48-07:35].
- Eye trace: cut between shots that put the point of interest in the same part of the frame so the viewer does not have to scan after each cut. Best for fast action [said 07:58-08:21].
- Quote from the Fury Road DP: the subject sits at frame centre because the viewer has only 1.8 seconds to find it at that cutting speed; the director's instruction was effectively "crosshairs on her nose". Some shots in that film are under a second [said 09:16-09:43].
- Split edit changes sound or picture before the other. J-cut: the audio of the next shot starts before we see it. L-cut: the previous audio carries on after the picture has cut away [said 10:14-10:58].
- Names come from the shape of the clips on a timeline [said 10:36].
- Split edits make dialogue feel natural, control the rhythm of the conversation and catch listeners' reaction shots [said 11:21].
- Creative use: while a character describes a plan, L-cut to what they describe (shows the plan, which is more interesting than hearing it); extra J/L cuts inside the dialogue keep the sequence moving [said 12:05-13:08].
- Intellectual montage (Kuleshov effect): unrelated images side by side make a meaning or feeling; the viewer connects them. It is a cut made more noticeable, not less [said 13:08-15:29].
- Cut on action: cut during a movement so the out point of shot A matches the in point of shot B; works for small actions (a drink, a handshake) and is used in nearly every genre for smoother, more natural cuts [said 15:49-16:32].
- In fights, cut on impact to give each hit force; place the cut mid-move; punch in from a wide to a close-up for drama [said 17:04-17:31].
- Left uncovered: freeze frames, invisible cuts, subliminal frames, double cuts, jump cuts (separate video) [said 17:56-18:17].

## Against today's playbook (smooth: cutsPerMinute 6, averageShotLength 10, zoomRamp 0.6, "let it breathe", "match moves across a cut")
- Confirms "Let it breathe": a contemplative scene holds shots longer, a frantic one cuts fast; pace serves emotion first [said 01:45]. No second figure is given, so it neither confirms nor refutes 10 s.
- Adds a craft the playbook lacks: J/L cuts (audio leads or trails the picture) on dialogue to make rhythm natural, and L-cut to illustrate what a speaker is describing [said 10:36-12:47]. The playbook has no audio-before-picture rule.
- Adds cut on action: out point of A matches the in point of B, cut mid-movement [said 15:49-16:10]. This pairs with "match moves across a cut" (the playbook's wording is about matching zoom speed; the tutor's is about matching the action itself).
- Eye trace (keep the subject at the same frame position across a cut) is a placement rule the playbook does not state. It is argued for fast action (1.8 s per shot is the quoted pace), so it is evidence for the fast end, not for a 10 s average [said 09:16].
- The only timing figure the video gives, 1.8 s, belongs to action editing and is far from the smooth fingerprint; do not use it for cinematic.
