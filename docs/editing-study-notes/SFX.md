## Rules stated
- A sound effect that is done well goes unnoticed; done badly it can ruin the video. Treat SFX with care, not as an afterthought [said 00:01].
- His method is called the FIN system: Find it, Incorporate it, Nail it [said 00:23].
- The bare minimum if you only want the mechanics: pull the effect in, trim it with In/Out (I and O keys), drag it to the timeline [said 00:43, again 03:07].
- Finding: type the most obvious word you can think of into a royalty-free library; it can take a while to land on the right one. Links to free sources are in the description [said 01:04, 02:05].
- Pick the effect by the emotion the scene needs. His example scene without any SFX "feels pretty damn empty"; he chooses a creepy tone for it [said 01:24, 01:44].
- Everyone should own a cinematic boom. Scrub through the file for a short one and trim to it [said 03:07].
- Never rely on one sound effect: "more often than not" one is not enough. He layers "layers upon layers" of effects in all his videos; layering is one of the most important parts of nailing it. Compared to mixing cocktails [said 03:27, 03:48].
- Speed/Duration is a creative tool: right-click the clip, Speed/Duration, tick Reverse. A reversed cinematic boom "works on pretty much anything" you put it on (a riser into the hit). Pitch and speed can also be altered but he leaves them alone for this effect [said 04:08 to 04:48].
- Place a long rising horror/build-up effect so that it ends exactly where the moment lands ("placing it at the end so the build-up works"), then add a second short sound (violin rise) on top as another layer [said 04:48, 05:29].
- Volume is the main clip setting to change. He says many gaming videos get SFX volume wrong, and that he did too [said 05:08].
- Loud, ear-blasting effects for easy laughs "sometimes really is" the way to go, but use it sparingly: after "three or four" blasts the viewer gets annoyed. This is the one numeric limit in the video [said 05:29].
- A sound that starts too harshly: right-click the start of the clip, Apply Default Transitions (audio fade in), drag the handle to set the length. It gives "a slow fade" at that end [said 05:49].
- Voice effects live in Premiere's Audio workspace (Essential Sound): Dialog tab presets such as "From Radio" and "From Television", reverb for an echoing voice, and a music section exists too [said 06:10 to 07:12].
- Music: he drops a royalty-free track under the radio scene with no level or fade stated ("blah blah you know how it is") [said 07:12].
- No dB values for effects, voice or music; no effects-per-minute figure; no meme length or placement rule; no music ducking rule [absent across whole transcript].

## Against today's playbook
- retention "Sound sells the hit": confirmed in spirit. He builds a rise into a hit (reversed boom, 24 s horror build ending in a violin stinger) [said 04:08, seen 05:50]. The playbook wording "a whoosh for movement, a hit on the reveal" has no counterpart in his stated rules, but "build-up ends where the payoff lands" is the same idea.
- retention.maxEffectsPerMinute 8 / effectsPerMinute 4: he gives no per-minute figure. His only cap is "three or four" ear-blast effects before the viewer tires [said 05:29]; that is a limit on loud effects in a run, not a rate. It supports a separate, lower cap on the loudest effects than on all effects.
- "Point at the joke, don't bury it": partly supported. He warns against sustained shouting-level effects; layering is encouraged but each layer is a different role (riser, hit, sting), not more of the same.
- Layering is a stated default (2 to 3 sounds per moment in his example: build-up, boom, violin). The playbook counts effects, so one "moment" with three layered sounds should count as one effect event, or the rate cap would be hit instantly [seen 05:50].
- Fade-in on a harsh effect start is something the auto-editor could copy: every riser or sting gets a default audio fade-in [said 05:49].
