## Rules stated
- Secret 1, audio first: start the edit with only audio, before touching a clip: voiceover, music and sound effects matter more than most think. For YouTube he records the hook and intro voiceover before shooting, finds music matching the energy in his head, adds a few sound effects, then sees where the voiceover is too long and gets new shot ideas he can still add to the shot list [said 01:21-02:22, 02:43].
- Example number: laying voiceover over music showed him that "7 seconds" to explain the viewer is overwhelmed was too long, so he made the build-up quicker; the track's rhythm inspired a paper-toss scene on the beat [said 02:02-02:43].
- After shooting, the edit is "halfway done": pacing, voiceover and music are in, only the shots remain to drop in [said 02:22-02:43].
- Secret 2, pacing: pace is the storytelling pulse; the biggest mistake even of experienced editors is staying at the same pace for too long [said 02:43-03:05].
- Three levers for pace: (a) shot length: short snappy cuts for speed/tension, longer shots for emotion or calm; (b) shot motion: chaotic handheld adds energy, smooth stable frames calm things down; (c) music and sound: a driving beat pushes forward, softer music lets it breathe, and sometimes silence is strong [said 03:05-03:48].
- Pace high all the time overwhelms, pace slow all the time bores; time the changes of energy because that snaps attention back or sets the emotion [said 03:48-04:09].
- His own 30-second hook is the worked example: no music with a rising sound effect, a pause for a second, then full energy with quick shots and fast music, a short break with a longer shot, tension building again, energy back up with fast cuts, release into slower shots with no music, then settle into a neutral pace [said 04:09-04:51].
- Mark your big moments and quiet moments, then let cuts, movement and music flow between them [said 04:51-05:11].
- Secret 3, assembly edit: do not polish early (he once spent two days on the first 30 s of a video and forgot what he was making). Rough shape first, like sketching before painting: spot the footage (cut out the junk, keep what you will use); drag clips into the timeline in rough story order with a beginning, middle and end; drop in the voiceover if recorded; find temp or real music matching the pace; play it all through, and see gaps, pacing problems, missing shots and what to remove [said 06:15-07:38].
- Secret 4, flow: cutting every clip on the beat feels nice but gets repetitive. Instead (1) cut intentionally, thinking where the eyes are on the screen and how they travel between shots; (2) beat accents: land an action on the beat (dropping something, stepping into frame, raising an eyebrow) instead of cutting on it; (3) audio transitions: a sound effect spanning two shots glues them, sometimes better than the picture [said 07:38-09:16].
- His intro example for flow: movement leaves the shot right to left, the next shot has it entering right to left, "fast but smooth"; then the actor's arrival at the desk and the eyebrow raise hit the beat [said 08:48-09:16].
- Secret 5, contrast and surprise: if the edit is predictable people tune out; break your own rhythm with a sudden cut, a scene change or something unexpected; even a small surprise is a spike of focus and these tiny spikes keep people watching. Contrast can be loud vs quiet, fast vs slow, bright vs dark [said 09:16-10:16]. Keep it part of the story, not forced; you need few, "one well-placed surprise" is enough (freeze frame, a quick silence, an unexpected cut) [said 10:16-10:58].
- Examples from his hook: a frozen frame with text dropped in before expected in the first three seconds, and later a cut to silence on the empty desk [said 10:16-10:38].
- Bonus, be ruthless: kill your darlings; make selections fast; cut what does not serve the story; the assembly edit forces quick decisions [said 11:43-12:28].
- Numbers he states: 15 years editing, "7 seconds", "two days" on the first 30 s, "first 30 seconds" hook, "first three seconds", 70% off sponsor code (not craft).
