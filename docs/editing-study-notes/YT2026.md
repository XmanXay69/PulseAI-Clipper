## Rules stated
- A gaming edit starts by picking out the juiciest parts; a challenge or vlog is cut chronologically so it is easy to follow and easy to voice over later [said 03:12-03:32]. In Premiere the + and - keys (timeline zoom) are called the key shortcut to learn [said 02:52-03:12].
- His "editing characteristics" are zooms, pacing and voice-over; voice-over over a quiet stretch is better than music-over-montage, which works but is "boring" [said 03:53-04:34].
- Intro: follow the "what, how and why" rule, and keep every intro animation "simple and smooth"; use elements that support the voice-over (a PNG that makes a transition, a character that points) then add flair on top [said 05:16-05:59]. Intro is the place for After Effects, 3D text, presets and sound design [said 05:16-05:38].
- Transitions: Premiere's default film-impact transitions only work on the clip layer; to also move text and PNGs, build the transition on adjustment layers above everything (a base preset of mirror effects on the bottom two layers, style preset on the top) [said 05:59-06:40].
- Animation starts with the Transform effect (position, scale, rotation; "rarely" the other fields) [said 07:01]. To slide a PNG in: set position off-screen, keyframe, then a second later reset to the default (centre) position as the second keyframe [said 07:22-07:42].
- Open the position keyframes' velocity graph and reshape the curve so the speed is "collected" at the start of the slide: this looks smoothest for things entering the screen [said 07:42-08:03]. For a pointing rotation: give position and rotation keyframes at the same times and make their velocity shapes as similar as possible: the more alike, the smoother the movement [said 08:03-08:24].
- Exit: add a keyframe where the exit starts and one where it ends, and mirror the shape of the entrance's final curve [said 08:24-08:44].
- The same keyframe thinking applies to text, arrows, PNGs, video layers; "little pop subtitles" via position/rotation keys on a title made with the T tool [said 08:44-09:05].
- Sound design deserves the same attention as visuals. He contrasts the same edit with "5 minutes" of SFX against "30 minutes" of SFX; audio is "the most underlooked part" and should be as detailed as the picture [said 09:27-10:12].
- Whoosh on every moving PNG or image; imagine everything moving through "incredibly thick air": entrance, sway, point, exit each makes a sound [said 10:12-10:33].
- Gain: press G in Premiere and change by 6 dB per step; he calls that the sweet spot for noticeable moves; do not hit the red [said 10:33-10:53].
- Sketch timing: for an intense, overwhelming scene overlap and interrupt the dialogue with J-cuts (audio leads, picture follows) [said 12:14-12:34]. For dramatic tension leave space after the big line: a held shot of the person taking it in before the reply [said 12:54-13:14]. No number for the gap.
- Letterbox: adjustment layer, Crop effect, top and bottom "about 15" (units not stated; Crop is in percent); keyframe both from 0 to 15 together, right-click the first keyframes for Ease Out and the last two for Ease In, which gives a smooth bars-in move [said 13:35-14:16]. "You can ease out and ease in on any keyframes."
- Tension audio is different from thick-air whooshes: risers, slow builds and a creeping ambient drone. The riser tells the viewer something big is next [said 14:57-15:45].
- Music choice is subjective: feel the scene, pick what fits [said 16:05-16:45]. Music is the foundation layer that sets pacing, cut timing and emotional tone [said 17:06-17:26].
- Cut to the beat: put cuts on the loudest notes (snare or kick). It is subtle but reliably makes an edit "seriously smooth"; deliberately off-beat cuts feel wrong [said 17:26-18:15]. Stopping the music for a moment and restarting adds tension [said 18:15]. An analog-film look suits an 80s-feeling track [said 18:15-18:36].
